Worked out on $100,000

  1. 01

    Treasuries

    The 10-year Treasury yields 5.28%, a level last seen in May 2002. $100,000 there earns about $5,280 a year until 2036.

    A 10-year Treasury note is a loan to the US government. It pays a fixed rate every six months for ten years, then hands your money back. Its yield closed at 5.28% on Oct 7, and touched 5.31% on Oct 5. Before this fall, it had not been that high since May 2002. It started 2026 at 4.18%, and its low was 0.52% on Aug 4, 2020. On Oct 7 the Treasury sold $39 billion of 10-year notes at 5.300%, and buyers bid for 2.77 times what was on offer, so people are buying at these rates.

    $100,000 at 5.28% → about $5,280 a year, $52,800 over 10 years with the interest paid out to you
    Reinvest the twice-a-year interest at the same rate → about $168,400 by 2036
    Same $100,000 at the August 2020 low of 0.52% → $520 a year
    Same $100,000 at the start of 2026 (4.18%) → $4,180 a year, $1,100 less than today
    3-month Treasury bills pay 4.22% → $4,220 a year, but that rate resets every three months; the 10-year fixes $1,060 a year more for a decade

    How to buy: in a brokerage account, look up Treasury notes maturing around 2036 and buy one; the yield your broker shows is what you lock in if you hold to the end. Or bid at auction: the Treasury sells new 10-year notes in February, May, August and November and reopens them eight times a year, and a noncompetitive bid through TreasuryDirect (from $100) or a broker gets you the auction rate. The interest is taxed federally but not by any state or city, which is worth a lot in California. For cash you will need sooner, see Savings; for more ideas, see Insights.

    The catch: the price moves while you hold it. If rates keep climbing and you sell before 2036, you can get back less than you paid; hold to the end and you get the full $100,000 back. The bigger risk is inflation: if prices rise faster than 5.28% a year over the decade, you lose buying power, and inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS) cover that at a lower starting rate. Notes sold below $100, like the Oct 7 batch (a 4.625% coupon at $94.86 per $100), pay part of their return as the price climbs back to $100, and the IRS taxes that part like interest. Rates could still go higher from here; nobody can call the top.
